# Academic taste

Status: design guidance. This file describes editorial judgment, not an automated quality guarantee.

## The standard

An academic slide earns attention by helping the audience understand a question, method, result, limitation, or decision. Visual polish supports that job. It cannot substitute for accurate evidence or a coherent argument.

Before choosing a layout, establish the audience, presentation setting, speaking time, central question, and what the audience should understand or do afterwards. Design the storyboard before the deck. Keep a usable path back to the source for each scientific claim; see [evidence-integrity.md](evidence-integrity.md).

## Editorial principles

1. **One intellectual job per slide.** Name the job in a sentence: “Compare the two estimates,” “Explain the sampling process,” or “Show where the result is uncertain.” A slide may contain several related elements when they jointly perform that job.
2. **Make the main message visible.** Prefer a precise assertion as the title when the evidence supports one. A question title is appropriate when the slide genuinely opens an inquiry. Methods and orientation slides may use a descriptive title. Never make a stronger title than the underlying result.
3. **Use evidence as the visual center.** Choose a chart, table, image, diagram, equation, or carefully set text because it carries information. A decorative photograph or icon is not a substitute for evidence. Some slides need no image.
4. **Design for the claim's complexity.** A dense methods diagram, multi-panel figure, or table may be necessary. Make its reading order clear and explain what to inspect. Splitting content is useful only when it preserves the needed comparison and context.
5. **Signal, then guide.** Use position, size, labels, and selective emphasis to direct attention. Animation is optional; reveal only when it improves explanation and the static state remains understandable.
6. **Be consistent without repeating a template.** Reuse typography, spacing, color roles, and citation treatment. Vary the layout to match the intellectual job rather than cycling through decorative variants.
7. **Design for the real display.** A good desktop preview can fail in a large room, on a video call, or as a PDF. Inspect rendered slides at presentation size and test in the intended setting when possible.

## Choose the visual form

| Intellectual job | Good starting form | Check before accepting |
|---|---|---|
| Show change or comparison | Directly labeled plot | Same scale, units, denominator, uncertainty, and comparison window |
| Explain a process or mechanism | Causal/process diagram with labeled arrows | Distinguish observed links from hypotheses or proposed mechanisms |
| Show a study design | Timeline, flow diagram, or compact table | Groups, inclusion/exclusion, time points, and missingness remain visible |
| Preserve exact values | Small table or labeled values | Precision and qualifiers match the source |
| Orient the audience | Recurring map or section marker | It clarifies the talk's structure rather than adding decoration |
| State a limitation or decision | Short, structured text | No visual is forced when text is clearest |

## Taste review

For each slide, ask:

- Can the audience identify its job within a few seconds?
- Is the focal element the evidence or explanation needed for that job?
- Does the title say exactly what the slide can support?
- Are labels, uncertainty, and limitations visible or deliberately handled in the spoken explanation and notes?
- Would removing any element improve clarity without changing the science?
- Does the slide still make sense when exported to PDF and viewed without animation?

If a simplification would hide uncertainty or change the claim, keep the complexity and improve the visual organization instead. Record unresolved design and scientific issues separately in the quality report.

## Basis and limits

This is product guidance, informed by research and expert guidance on scientific presentations and slide assessment, not a claim that one layout improves every talk. The [AWSM slide rubric study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9005758/) reports that medical educators prioritized figure readability and low visual busyness. The [scientific presentation guide by Grambow and Weinfurt](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12826339/) discusses audience, data orientation, and delivery across formats. Apply these ideas with judgment; neither source validates an individual deck.
